Who Gets Care and Who Doesn't? Migrants and Healthcare Access Under Climate Stress in Kenya and South Africa

A comparative qualitative study of how climate pressure, mobility, documentation, and frontline decision-making shape access to public healthcare in Kenya and South Africa.

📅 Published: June 2026·👥 Dr Margaret Monyani & Damaris Lena Oluga·📄 25 pages·62 participants
⬇ Download PDF Read Online

Jointly produced by OLAM Africa Research Institute and Migration Pulse Hub. Fieldwork was conducted from January to April 2026 in Nairobi, Kakuma, Kalobeyei, Dadaab, Gauteng, Johannesburg, and KwaZulu-Natal.

Key Findings

  • In Kenya, access often breaks down when refugee documentation is not recognised across health, insurance, and digital systems.
  • In South Africa, formal entitlement is unevenly applied, so similar patients can receive different treatment depending on the facility or staff member.
  • Across both countries, cost, documentation barriers, and fear of rejection cause migrants and refugees to delay or avoid care.
  • Climate-related floods, droughts, and extreme heat increase demand while leaving existing access barriers unchanged.
  • The report calls for health systems planned around mobility, integrated refugee healthcare, consistent frontline standards, and aligned climate, migration, and health policy.

Refugee Week Kenya Cross-Culture Festival 2026 Report

The official report from the inaugural national Refugee Week celebration in Kenya, co-convened by Migration Pulse Hub and Umoja Refugee Creatives.

📅 17–19 June & 3 July 2026·📍 University of Nairobi & hybrid webinar·📄 36 pages
⬇ Download Report View Festival Highlights

Impact at a Glance

  • 300–500 participants attended each festival day, with 700–1,200 refugees reached.
  • 102 households were supported with Social Health Authority registration.
  • Sixteen refugee-led organisations and seventeen partner organisations took part.
  • Sixteen exhibitors showcased art, crafts, digital technology, fashion, and culinary enterprise.
  • The programme concluded with a 3 July hybrid webinar marking the 75th anniversary of the 1951 Refugee Convention.

Ending Statelessness in East Africa: Legislative Gaps and Reform Pathways

A comparative analysis of nationality laws across eight East African countries, with targeted legislative reform recommendations for each jurisdiction.

📅 Published: November 2025·👥 Authors: MPH Legal Team·📄 28 pages
⬇ Request PDF 📋 Request Summary

PDFs are available free of charge on request — email us at info@migrationpulsehub.org.

Citation: Migration Pulse Hub, Ending Statelessness in East Africa, 2025, migrationpulsehub.org

Key Findings

  • Six of eight countries still have gender-discriminatory nationality laws that prevent mothers from conferring citizenship to their children.
  • An estimated 700,000 people in East Africa are stateless — the majority from pastoralist communities whose nomadic movement crosses administrative boundaries.
  • Only Kenya and Uganda have ratified both the 1954 Convention Relating to the Status of Stateless Persons and the 1961 Convention on the Reduction of Statelessness.
  • Birth registration rates among stateless communities average 23% — compared to a regional average of 61% — creating generational statelessness.

Detained Without Rights: Immigration Detention Practices in Kenya and the Case for Reform

Based on 18 months of detention monitoring and 140 interviews with migrants, this report documents systemic violations and proposes a detention reform agenda.

📅 Published: June 2025·👥 Authors: MPH Legal & Research Teams·📄 58 pages
⬇ Request PDF 📋 Request Summary

PDFs are available free of charge on request — email us at info@migrationpulsehub.org.

Citation: Migration Pulse Hub, Detained Without Rights, 2025, migrationpulsehub.org

Key Findings

  • Average detention period without judicial review: 47 days — in violation of Kenya's constitutional requirement for review within 24 hours.
  • 78% of detained migrants had no access to legal counsel during their detention period.
  • Children were detained alongside adults in 6 of 8 monitored facilities, in direct violation of the Children's Act and international law.
  • No formal complaints mechanism exists in any of the monitored facilities — leaving detained migrants with no recourse for abuse or mistreatment.
